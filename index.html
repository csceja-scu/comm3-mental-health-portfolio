Chapter 01

The catalyst

Three lives lost.
One campus forced to respond.

My work began with a hard question: how should a student tell the story of a campus mental-health crisis without reducing it to statistics or spectacle?

For this COMM 3 portfolio, I connected research, an interview with former SCU journalism senior lecturer Barbara Kelley, and visual storytelling to document how grief, academic pressure, and student advocacy shaped the university's response.

Perspective

As an SCU student, veteran, first-generation Mexican American, and tech worker, I approached this project with attention to pressure and systems: the personal pressure students carry, and the institutional systems responsible for meeting students before crisis.

  1. Fall 2021

    Three student deaths affect the SCU community. The deaths, including two suicides, left students grieving and looking for answers.

  2. Afterward

    Students organized and demanded stronger mental-health resources. The movement focused attention on Counseling and Psychological Services, known as CAPS.

  3. Response

    SCU allocated millions to improve CAPS. The response became part of a larger conversation about care, access, and the pressure students face.

Chapter 02

The evidence

The hidden weight of a generation.

While these deaths were individual tragedies, their impact was amplified by larger patterns in higher education: distress, academic pressure, and a gap between need and support-seeking. To frame the problem, this portfolio references a 2021 study of 23,307 higher-education students one year into the COVID-19 pandemic.

  • 50.6%

    Presented anxiety symptoms

  • 55.1%

    Presented depressive symptoms

  • 20.8%

    Reported suicidal ideation

  • 33.3%

    Had consulted or intended to consult a psychologist

Source: Schmits et al. (2021), Psychological Distress among Students in Higher Education.

Chapter 03

Primary reporting

The interview made the climate real.

Barbara Kelley taught journalism at Santa Clara for 27 years. Her interview gave this project a direct account of the fear, grief, and frustration students carried after the deaths.

Read the full transcript

Charlie Gray (narrator): The following audio story contains themes related to mental health and suicide. Viewer discretion is advised.

Charlie Gray (narrator): When I arrived as a freshman to Santa Clara University, I was almost immediately funneled into a large freshman orientation. Curiously, though, almost two-thirds of the programming centered around a very extensive mental health support network. There was a 24-7 hotline, multiple counselors, therapists living in each residence hall, and even matching friendship bracelets that were supposed to foster connection amongst the student body.

The person next to me mentioned this was by design. According to him, the previous year, a student tragically ended his own life, leading to outrage amongst the student population.

We wanted to explore this moment in the school's history, to tell the story of this death and how it impacted Santa Clara University's approach to mental health. What we found instead was a series of tragic incidents that revealed a deep, systemic problem within our university.

My co-producer, Carlos, tracked down a former SCU professor to get the story.

Barbara Kelley (interviewee): Yeah, I was a full-time journalism teacher at Santa Clara for 27 years, and I just retired. I taught journalism that whole time, and before and during part of that time, I was also working as a journalist.

The students were really traumatized when the first one happened. There were a few students who were pretty good friends with the first suicide, and the student who killed himself, excuse me for being graphic, he was hit by a train on the tracks by the high school. This one student who knew him really well from high school and at Santa Clara, she was just heartbroken. She was shocked. In the other class, there were several students who didn't want to come to class after they found out because they were so upset, and it was really tough for them to snap back into school mode.

And then there was another death. Nobody really knew exactly what happened, but it was a frat boy walking home from a party at a club in downtown San Jose, and he was found underneath an overpass, apparently, but nobody knew for sure, but it looked like he had fallen. And then there were students that came to class after that because they knew him pretty well, and they were having a hard time concentrating.

And then the third suicide was just horrific. But a young man threw himself out the window, ninth floor, Swig, and almost everyone was affected by it.

One of the things the university did that the students just found so outrageous, they sent out this form letter, you know, with a picture of a candle, and, you know, "we regret the news," blah, blah, blah. But no details, and, yeah, maybe you don't want to put the details in something like that, but most of the students knew, and they wanted it acknowledged.

Charlie Gray (narrator): The students on campus, already under a heavy course load, felt the university's response was inadequate. They felt that the university not only would not give students the time and space to heal from this incident, but they didn't even want to talk about the traumatic deaths.

Barbara Kelley (interviewee): Two days later, the Associated Students organized a big rally, and it was the biggest one I've ever seen since I've taught at Santa Clara, but just open mics, students speaking their mind: let's acknowledge what happened, and talk about it, and try and process it.

Carlos Ceja (interviewer): What was the feeling being there? What was the general sentiment within the crowd?

Barbara Kelley (interviewee): From the students, it was kind of a universal feeling, that yes, I hear what you're saying, I'm there too. I think people were really unified in that, and there's a lot of solidarity.

Carlos Ceja (interviewer): What do you feel like is kind of the main takeaway from, you know, the events that transpired during that time?

Barbara Kelley (interviewee): Just to be honest, you know, bad things happen, and you can't sweep them under the rug, or you can't use euphemisms, you know? In 27 years, that is the worst that had ever happened on campus. And I had heard from one of my students, and she said, "Did you know that over the past year, there have been six suicides on campus, none of which had been acknowledged?" Let's not ignore a problem like this.

Charlie Gray (narrator): According to the Santa Clara University newspaper, shortly after the demonstrations ended, the university's board of trustees committed several million dollars to mental health resources. In a press release issued this year, the university stated, quote, "In the last few years, Counseling and Psychological Services, CAPS, has expanded its services with a greater number of diverse hires, 24-7 virtual therapy options, new student therapy groups, private on-campus wellness spaces, and more."

This audio story was created by Carlos Ceja and me, Charlie Gray, for Santa Clara University's Communication 3 class. Special thanks to Professor Barbara Kelley for sharing her perspective with us, and to Professor Kratz for her guidance throughout this project.

Visual story

Place, pressure, and memory.

The original visual project by Carlos Ceja and Charlie Gray used campus and nearby locations to help viewers imagine and reflect on what happened. This web version presents key moments natively, with the full PDF preserved as the archive artifact.

Artist statement

In this visual piece, we intend to represent the impact of these deaths through the scenes where they had impact or occurred — allowing our audience to imagine and reflect on these events. As SCU students creating this story, we empathize with the struggles these Broncos faced. We hope to represent this story thoughtfully through the eyes of fellow Broncos, for Broncos.
